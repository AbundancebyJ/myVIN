Look at almost any shareholders' agreement (SHA) for an equity investment and you'll find two clauses: the **tag-along right** and the **drag-along right**. The names are easy to mix up, but they protect opposite sides.

## In one line

- **Tag-along**: when the majority holder sells, **you can sell alongside on the same terms**. It protects minority holders.
- **Drag-along**: when you sell, **you can require the other holders to sell on the same terms**. It makes a sale possible.

| | Tag-along | Drag-along |
|---|---|---|
| Protects | Minority shareholders | The shareholder driving the sale |
| Nature | An **option** to join | A **power** to compel |
| Problem it solves | The majority holder exits alone and keeps the control premium | Holdouts block a 100% sale |
| Typical use | Minority investments by SIs and FIs | Sales where the buyer wants 100%; FI exits |

## How a tag-along works

The usual sequence:

1. The majority holder agrees to sell to a third party and **notifies** the other shareholders of the terms: price, number of shares, payment terms.
2. Each minority holder **elects** within a set period (often a few weeks) whether to tag along.
3. If it does, the buyer must buy the minority stake **at the same price and on the same terms**, usually pro rata to the portion the majority holder sells.
4. If the buyer won't take the minority shares, the majority holder can't complete the sale, or can sell only a reduced number.

The point is that **the majority holder can't keep the control premium to itself.** A change of control can leave minority holders stuck with an owner they didn't choose; the tag-along gives them an exit at the same price.

### What gets negotiated

- **Trigger**: any sale by the majority holder, or only a change of control
- **Pro rata vs full tag**: on a change of control, minority holders may ask the buyer to take all their shares
- **What "same terms" covers**: whether the control premium is in the per-share price, and whether the seller gets anything on the side (employment, consulting fees)
- **Carve-outs**: permitted transfers such as intra-group moves or pledges usually don't trigger it

## How a drag-along works

A drag-along gives power to **the side that wants to sell**. Buyers often want 100%: leftover minority holders complicate decisions and gain leverage. With a drag-along, a qualifying shareholder who sells can require the others to sell on the same terms.

In Korea, **financial investors taking minority stakes** often ask for a drag-along as an exit route: if the company hasn't gone public by a deadline, the FI can sell the whole company, majority stake included. These structures have ended up in court. The best-known case is the investment in Doosan Infracore China (DICC): after the IPO fell through, the financial investors exercised their drag-along right, and a dispute over the company's duty to cooperate with the sale turned into years of litigation.

### What gets negotiated

- **Conditions**: a minimum shareholding or consent threshold, a price or return floor, a time limit or failed IPO
- **Same terms**: dragged holders get the same price and the same form of consideration (for example, cash)
- **Liability caps**: dragged holders' representations, warranties and indemnities limited to their pro-rata share and proceeds
- **Buyer limits**: no drag into a sale to the seller's affiliates, so nobody is dragged into a cheap insider deal
- **Duty to cooperate**: spell out what the company and majority holder must do (data room access, for example); vague wording invites disputes at exercise

## Clauses that travel together

Tag-along and drag-along usually sit next to a **right of first refusal (ROFR)**. The typical order: a selling shareholder first offers the stake to the others on the same terms; if they pass, the stake can go to a third party, and that is when tag-along or drag-along applies. If the clauses' notice periods don't line up, a sale can slip by months, so it pays to align them up front.

## A strategic investor's checklist

When a strategic investor (SI) takes a minority stake, the usual questions are:

- Do we have a **tag-along or ROFR** in case the majority holder sells to a competitor?
- If financial investors are in the deal, **can their drag-along pull our stake into a sale**, and do we get a consent right or first refusal?
- What happens to our **commercial agreements** (supply, licensing) if a drag-along is exercised?

## Finding these terms in filings

The full SHA is often not public, but filings leave clues. Korean listed companies sometimes summarize key terms in stake-acquisition disclosures or registration statements, and US companies sometimes file shareholder or investment agreements as exhibits to SEC filings (8-K, S-1, 10-K).
